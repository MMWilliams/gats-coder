## 3. Harnesses grow, then models absorb them

_2:30-4:00 · 148 words · ~1:03 of 1:30_

[Read the lanes top to bottom.] A harness is everything around the model: what goes into its context, how it reasons, what tools it can call, what it's allowed to do, and how we check the result. Since 2020, harnesses kept adding jobs. Context: prompts, retrieval, memory. Reasoning: chain of thought, voting, reflection, tree search. Tools: the ReAct loop, function calling, MCP, and now Claude Code, Codex and Antigravity. Permission: from AutoGPT asking yes or no, to today's permission modes and sandboxes. Then reasoning models, o1 and R1, trained the whole reasoning lane into the weights. That's why the big coding agents run a simple loop with no tree search. What a model can't absorb is contact with the world: what's true right now, what it's allowed to touch, and what actually happened. Look at the bottom lane. Checking and recording what happened is the thinnest lane, and it's what this talk is about.

## 4. Today’s harness is tomorrow’s model

_4:00-5:15 · 137 words · ~0:58 of 1:15_

Why does this keep happening? A harness produces training data. Its traces become training examples and its checks become rewards, so whatever procedure the harness runs, the next model learns. Left side, already happened. 'Think step by step' became reasoning models. Voting became self-checking. ReAct's text format became native tool calls. Tree search became learned backtracking; you can watch R1 write 'wait' and back up. Right side is my guess for the next round: context compaction, subagents, skills and rules, and plan search, including mine. GATS's planner is a procedure, so it may get absorbed too. What can't move inside is the check. Once a model trains against a verifier, the verifier becomes a target, and on ImpossibleBench stronger models game tests more. So the parts worth building outside the model are sensors, gates and the record.

## 5. Task length doubles about every 4 months

_5:15-6:45 · 159 words · ~1:08 of 1:30_

METR measures the length of task, in human time, that an agent finishes half the time. Since 2023 it doubles about every four months. GPT-2: seconds. GPT-4: a few minutes. Claude 3.7: about an hour. Opus 4.6: around twelve hours. Each jump came with a harness change: tool loops got us to minutes, reasoning models plus agent harnesses got us to hours. If the trend holds, a work week lands around the end of this year and a work month around late 2027. That's an extrapolation, not a promise, and past sixteen hours their task suite stops being reliable. My prediction: after a month, the next step isn't a longer task. It's a different kind of request. Tasks end. Responsibilities don't: keep prod healthy, run on-call, maintain a codebase. There's no finish line, only conditions you keep true. That's not a cron job or a pipeline. It's one agent that keeps its own state and decides what needs doing.

## 6. Seven gaps that limit long-horizon agents

_6:45-8:45 · 196 words · ~1:24 of 2:00_

So what stops that line? I see seven gaps, each with a number. Reliability: METR's numbers are at 50% success. At 80%, the horizon is five to ten times shorter; for Mythos, 17 hours drops to 3. And 99% per step is only 37% after 100 steps. Trust: on ImpossibleBench, GPT-5 passed 76% of the impossible tasks in one SWE-bench variant by gaming the tests. Auditability: the EU AI Act's Article 12 requires high-risk systems to log events over their lifetime, starting December 2027. A chat transcript isn't tamper-evident and can't be replayed. Reproducibility: 1,000 runs of the same prompt at temperature zero gave 80 different answers. Planning: o1-preview scores 98% on Blocksworld, and about 24% once plans need 20 to 40 steps. World models: an agent deleted a production database during a code freeze, then said rollback was impossible. Rollback worked. Groundedness is the one with a known fix: AlphaGeometry solved 25 of 30 olympiad problems by having a symbolic engine check the neural model's guesses. A better model shrinks all of these. It doesn't remove them. They're properties of the system around the model, and that's where I think the next big gain is. [If asked: METR caught o3 reward hacking in 30% of its RE-Bench runs.]

## 7. If the gaps closed

_8:45-10:00 · 109 words · ~0:46 of 1:15_

Suppose those gaps closed. Start with arithmetic. At 99% per step, 100 steps succeed 37% of the time. Add a check after every step that catches nine errors in ten, and the error rate per step drops to one in a thousand: 100 steps now succeed 90% of the time. Same model. That's math, not a benchmark. Close the other gaps and you get horizons you can quote at 99% instead of 50%, regression tests for agent behavior, and agents that regulated industries can actually use. Put together: an agent you can leave running on a responsibility, not just a task. Next, how GATS and LEDGER go after these.

## 8. The LLM is the last resort

_10:00-11:15 · 149 words · ~1:03 of 1:15_

GATS is the planner, from my paper with Dymitr Nowicki. Tree search over a graph of states that persists between runs picks every action. When it needs to predict what an action will do, it asks the cheapest layer that can answer: symbolic contracts first, exact and free; then statistics from its own past runs; and only for a transition it has never seen, a cached LLM call. Think of two sailors. Today's agents are skilled sailors without a chart. They read the sea and estimate where they are, and the error grows every mile. GATS has a chart. It plans the route before leaving, checks its position at every waypoint, and corrects the chart when the sea disagrees, so it doesn't hit the same rock twice. On a 12-category stress test it scores 100%, against 89% for LATS and 24% for ReAct, with zero LLM calls during planning. [Say 'during planning' every time you say zero: 50 one-time bootstrap calls. If asked about world models: Vafa et al., NeurIPS 2024.]

## 9. Keep the search space small

_11:15-12:00 · 70 words · ~0:30 of 0:45_

It has a hard limit. Tree search over raw actions breaks down past about 20 actions or depth 10. So LEDGER searches only a handful of coarse actions: run the suite, write a patch, apply it, commit, open a PR. The open-ended part, writing the code, goes to the LLM inside one step. The model never picks the next move. It fills in a step whose result we can measure.

## 10. Give the model zero trust

_12:00-14:30 · 238 words · ~1:42 of 2:30_

LEDGER is the experiment: how reliable can a coding agent get if the model gets zero trust? Most agents let the model decide what to do, do it, check its own work and write the report. LEDGER takes all of that away except one job: writing the patch. [Walk the pipeline.] GATS plans every action with zero model calls. The LLM writes a patch, nothing else. Sensors decide what's true. The journal records everything, hash-chained. Three rules. It can't set state: whether tests pass, whether the repo is clean, whether a step can be undone, all of that comes from sensors. It can't claim success: if what it says it did doesn't match what the sensors see, that's logged as an incident and the planner routes around it. It can't pick or run actions: GATS picks them, risky ones go through gates, and when it's stuck it escalates with a named reason, like 'infeasible'. The cost is flexibility. It does far less than Claude Code. The question is how much reliability that buys. [Point at the terminal.] This also answers the title. What did my agent actually do? I ask the record, not the model. 'ledger why' shows the search table it used to pick step one, not an explanation written afterward. 'ledger replay' reproduces the run bit for bit. And if anyone edits one field of the log, 'verify-chain' catches it. It runs on Claude, OpenAI or a local model with the same guarantees. [If asked: irreversible means an action that deletes the 'recoverable' proposition; ties break alphabetically, no randomness; API keys never reach the repo's own processes.]

## 12. The model is a part you swap

_16:30-19:30 · 279 words · ~1:59 of 3:00_

Where does this go? First: in a company, the model is a part you'll swap every few months. I swapped Claude for a local 27B model and the guarantees didn't move, because they live in the harness: the state, the checks, the record. Second: the harness is already an RL environment. Sensors are the reward signal, worktrees are the rollouts, and the journal holds the trajectories. Every action has a checked outcome, so you know which step earned the reward. Third: self-improvement, with a gate. 'ledger improve' files its own bugs as failing tests and fixes them, using only a local model. The gate caught 2 bad changes out of 5. That's only safe while the checker sits outside the thing being improved. [Bottom row.] You don't need LEDGER to use this on Monday. Two rules first. Context degrades as it fills: 40 files in, the agent ignores the rule you gave it at the start. And without a pass/fail check, the agent stops when the work looks done, and you become the checker. So, four habits. One: give every task a runnable check. Write the failing test first and ask it to end with the test output. Two: treat context as a budget. One task per session, clear between tasks, send investigation to subagents, and write a handoff file when a session gets long. Three: before you call it done, get a review from a fresh context: a review command, a second session, or another model, asking only for correctness gaps. Four: when you correct the same thing twice, make it permanent. A judgment call goes in AGENTS.md, a repeated workflow becomes a skill, a mechanical rule becomes a hook. [Full prompts and the verification ladder are in backup. If asked: one accepted self-improvement commit was partly gamed.]

## 13. Confidence is not evidence.

_19:30-21:00 · 91 words · ~0:39 of 1:30_

[Read the line. Pause.] Confidence is not evidence. An agent saying 'tests pass' is a claim, not a fact. So where does this go? One: agent evals will report variance and whether a run can be replayed, not just a success rate. Two: regulated work will require deterministic replay of what an agent did. Three: the LLM moves down the stack. It writes the code inside a step, and the checks around it are where products differ. Four: 'I can't do this as specified' becomes a capability we score, instead of a failure we ignore.

## 14. We can only prompt what we can foresee

_21:00-23:00 · 212 words · ~1:30 of 2:00_

Last thought. We can only prompt what we can foresee. Try writing thirty scenarios into a prompt: you'll cover the five you thought of, and the agent fills in the rest with guesses, then grades itself. As tasks turn into responsibilities, there's no final outcome to specify, only states to keep. So we stop giving steps and finish lines, and give intent instead: the purpose, the conditions to keep true, limits on what it may do, a budget, and when to come back. [Point at the loop.] This is the loop the agent runs, and it never ends. Sense what's true. Compare it to the state it should hold. Simulate options and pick one. Act, with irreversible steps gated and everything logged. It comes back to you only on exceptions: a condition at risk, a limit hit, or 'this can't be done as specified'. Working with it looks like managing. You review plans and exceptions, not every step, and you change the intent, not the instructions. Round trips are already a hidden cost: in METR's 2025 study, experienced developers were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they were 20% faster. So stop writing better prompts. Write intent an agent can check itself against, and keep the record that lets you check it too. Thank you.

## 7. If the gaps closed

_10:00-11:30 · 177 words · ~1:15 of 1:30_

- 99%/step over 100 steps: 37%

- The 37% cliff moves from step 100 to step 1,000: catch rate r multiplies the horizon by 1/(1 - r); 90% is 10x, 99% is 100x
- Condition: the check fails independently of the model; self-grading misses its own mistakes
- Unlocks: horizons quoted at 99%, regression tests for behavior, agents regulated industries can audit

Script:

Suppose those gaps closed. Start with arithmetic. At 99% per step, 100 steps succeed 37% of the time. Now add a check after every step that catches nine errors in ten. The error rate drops from one in a hundred to one in a thousand, and 100 steps succeed 90% of the time. Same model. Put another way, the 37% cliff moves from step 100 to step 1,000. A check that catches 90% buys a ten times longer horizon; one that catches 99% buys a hundred times. That's math, not a benchmark, and it has one condition: the check has to fail independently of the model. A model grading its own work tends to miss the same things it got wrong. That's why the check lives outside. Close the other gaps and you get horizons you can quote at 99% instead of 50%, regression tests for agent behavior, and agents regulated industries can actually audit. ## 8. The LLM is the last resort

_11:30-13:30 · 249 words · ~1:46 of 2:00_

Beats:

- NYC taxi transformer (Vafa et al., NeurIPS 2024): near-perfect directions, impossible map; close 1% of streets, ~100% to 67%
- Local: some of those rides probably passed this building
- Today's agents: skilled sailors without a chart. GATS has a chart.
- UCB1 tree search over a persistent state graph picks every action; score = average result + bonus for rarely tried options
- Predictions from the cheapest layer: symbolic contracts, then its own statistics, then a cached LLM for new transitions only
- Fixes the chart when the sea disagrees; a repeat trip makes no LLM calls
- Stress test, 12 categories: GATS 100%, LATS 89%, ReAct 24%; 0 LLM calls during planning (50 one-time bootstrap); 0 variance

Script:

GATS is the planner, from my paper with my co-author, Dymitr Nowicki. Here's the problem it's built for. A NeurIPS 2024 paper trained a transformer on New York taxi rides; some of them probably drove past this building. It gave valid turn-by-turn directions through Manhattan almost every time. Then the authors pulled out the map it had learned: streets that don't exist, overpasses across the grid. Close 1% of the streets, and accuracy fell from nearly 100% to 67%. It learned routes, not the city. Today's agents are skilled sailors without a chart. They read the sea and estimate where they are, and the error grows every mile. GATS has a chart. [Point at the layers.] Tree search picks every action, over a graph of states that persists between runs. It uses UCB1, which scores each option by its average result plus a bonus for options it hasn't tried much. To predict what an action will do, it asks the cheapest layer that can answer. Symbolic contracts first: exact and free. Then statistics from its own past runs. Only for a transition it's never seen, a cached LLM call. It plans the route before leaving, checks its position at every waypoint, and fixes the chart when the sea disagrees, so it doesn't hit the same rock twice. A repeat trip makes no LLM calls at all. On a 12-category stress test it scores 100%, against 89% for LATS and 24% for ReAct, with zero LLM calls during planning and zero variance across seeds. [Say 'during planning' every time you say zero: 50 one-time bootstrap calls.]

## 10. Give the model zero trust

Script:

LEDGER is the experiment: how reliable can a coding agent get if the model gets zero trust? In most agents the model decides what to do, does it, checks its own work and writes the report. LEDGER takes all of that away except one job: writing the patch. [Walk the pipeline.] GATS plans every action. The LLM writes a patch, nothing else. Sensors decide what's true. The journal records everything. Three rules. It can't set state: whether tests pass, whether the repo is clean, whether a step can be undone, all of that comes from sensors. It can't claim success: if what it says doesn't match what the sensors see, that's logged as an incident and the planner routes around it. It can't pick or run actions: GATS picks them, risky ones go through gates, and when it's stuck it escalates with a named reason, like 'infeasible'. The cost is flexibility. It does far less than Claude Code. The question is how much reliability that buys. [Point at the terminal.] This also answers the title. What did my agent actually do? 'ledger replay' reproduces the run bit for bit. Each journal entry carries the hash of the one before it, so if anyone edits one field, 'verify-chain' shows where the chain breaks. Same guarantees on Claude, OpenAI or a local model. I swapped Claude for a local 27B model and the guarantees didn't move, because they live in the harness: the state, the checks, the record. Second: the harness is already an RL environment. ## 14. We can only prompt what we can foresee

_22:00-24:15 · 272 words · ~1:56 of 2:15_

Beats:

- We can only prompt what we can foresee: write 30 scenarios and you cover the 5 you thought of; it guesses the rest and grades itself

- The loop: sense, compare to the target state, simulate and pick, act (irreversible steps gated, all logged)
- Back to you only on exceptions: condition at risk, limit hit, can't be done as specified
- Round trips cost: METR study, 16 experienced devs, 246 tasks, 19% slower with AI, believed 20% faster
- Close: stop writing better prompts; write intent it can check itself against, and keep the record
- Invite: try LEDGER or break it, and tell me

Script:

Last thought. We can only prompt what we can foresee. Try writing thirty scenarios into a prompt. You'll cover the five you thought of, the agent fills in the rest with guesses, and then it grades itself. As tasks turn into responsibilities, there's no final outcome to specify, only states to keep. Ships already work this way. A captain doesn't hand the officer of the watch a script. They leave standing orders: the course to hold, the limits, and when to call me, like another ship passing within two miles. Everything else is the watch's call, and it all goes in the log. That's what we give agents next: intent. The purpose, the conditions to keep true, limits on what it may do, a budget, and when to come back. [Point at the loop.] This is the loop it runs, and it never ends. Sense what's true. Compare it to the state it should hold. Simulate options and pick one. Act, with irreversible steps gated and everything logged. It comes back only on exceptions: a condition at risk, a limit hit, or 'this can't be done as specified'. Working with it looks like managing. You review plans and exceptions, not every step, and you change the intent, not the instructions. Round trips are already a hidden cost. In METR's 2025 study, 16 experienced open-source developers were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they were 20% faster. So stop writing better prompts. Write intent an agent can check itself against, and keep the record that lets you check it too. If you try LEDGER, or break it, I'd really like to hear about it. Thank you.
